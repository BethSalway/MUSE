## VR Possibilities for Educational Museum Programs and Games

Current museum VR experiences are insufficient and do not reflect the true possibilities of integrating this technology within museum programming. Since approximately 2016 museums have begun using VR technologies in a number of ways. This includes within the physical museum space as an aspect of a physical exhibit and remote exhibit access to those with headsets at home. The first example has been more successful than the latter; however, I believe it is the latter that holds more potential.

Current remote exhibits have been largely unsuccessful because they are low quality experiences. In researching this topic I explored a number of these VR experiences offered by museums within the United States, and the Louvre, located in Paris, France. I will be using the Louvre experience and the experience from the Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History to illustrate the range of what I found.

The Louvre VR experience reflects the majority of what I ran into during this process. When attempting to start the VR experience it was not only extremely difficult to access, but it also simply would not start. I followed the launch instructions on multiple different headsets, including a Meta Quest 2, a Vive focus 3, and a Vive Pro Eye. None of which were able to successfully launch the experience, furthermore I was unable to find any information stating what kind of headset I ought to use or whether or not there was any reason the program could not run in my region, device, etc. Unfortunately, this was the case for most of the experiences I attempted. Meaning I spent a significant amount of my time pressing buttons in vain.

On the other end of the spectrum are the experiences I was successful in launching, including the Smithsonian. These experiences were disappointing, to say the least. In regards to the Smithsonian experience, I was able to both launch the experience and interact with the space, clicking buttons, teleport, etc. That being said, there were still multiple technical issues. It looked to be made up of 360 poorly stitched photographs of the exhibit space. The way in which these photos were placed in the virtual reality space made the entire exhibit larger than life, by a noticeable degree. The plaques and blurbs accompanying objects were entirely unreadable and many of the buttons produced confusing results. The Smithsonian experience was the only one I could find that included any audio, pressing a button at the entrance to the exhibit started an audio clip. At first I was ecstatic, an audio tour in a virtual exhibit felt very promising. But, after a few short minutes of multiple voices talking about the quality and revolutionary aspects of the exhibit ahead, the audio abruptly stopped, leaving me in a dissatisfying silence. The other buttons scattered throughout the exhibit, located next to objects and display cases, were extremely confusing, in that they technically function, but in the most inconvenient way possible. When a button was clicked while in the VR space it seemed to do nothing, but when I stepped out of the VR I found that the buttons had opened blurbs of information on the computer screen. So, to access the educational information I had to press a VR button then take off the headset and read it, or I had to have another person in the room reading the information to me. Neither option was appealing. These two examples encompass the range of remote museum VR offerings that I was personally able to find over the course of two months. There are multiple examples for VR experiences of outdoor cultural heritage sites developed by non-profits like CyArk, but indoor spaces have been neglected.

Keeping in mind the cost of developing these experiences between 2016 to 2021, the period in which many were made, this is not all that surprising. Individuals with the skills of development have been and are being hired competitively by game and entertainment studios which leaves a gap for the GLAM (Galleries, Libraries, Archives, and Museums) industry. Making it difficult for them to integrate technologies and develop VR experiences due to cost. According to an article in The International Archives of the Photogrammetry, Remote Sensing and Spatial Information Sciences,  “the GLAM sector faces significant challenges in recruiting skilled workers for their digital teams capable of developing such technologies for cultural heritage presentation.”1. Paired with hardware and software costs museums simply could not afford to take advantage of these technologies to their fullest potential. But, the fast trajectory of the technological progress our society is experiencing has changed this within a matter of years. Software costs are now far more affordable and less hardware is required for development, meaning although labor is still the biggest cost the total project cost is far less. Software like Unity and Unreal Engine are great options. Unity requires a subscription be purchased if an organization's total revenue and funding exceed $200,000 USD, making the cost $210 a month per seat. And Unreal Engine requires 5% royalties if a project earns more than $1 Million USD. Furthermore, the software Quidient allows for anyone with a mobile device to digitize objects and scenes with amazing quality. Their Reality ModelTM Services provide an alternative to purchasing the software by converting videos of an object into high quality 3D models. So, by using Quidient museums can avoid the cost of lidar, lasers, or another expensive piece of hardware. AI tools may also support project costs, as pattern matching systems they are adept at assisting programming professionals. AI is incapable of generating the whole project for you, but it could mean museums don’t need to hire as many people to complete a VR exhibit or game.

Needed hardware costs vary, headsets may cost anywhere between $300 to $5000, but even just one or two headsets can be sufficient. Additionally, many museums already own or have access to hardware and software because of past projects. And, as an alternative option, outsourcing the project to a development company may be feasible. Companies like Northrop Grumman have developed VR experiences of cultural heritage sites in the past. VR developer Ryan Salway suggested without outsourcing the project a VR experience could be developed using the above software for as little as $10,000 to $75,000 USD2. The cost would go up depending on how many exhibits a museum digitizes and develops, so a long term program as I will propose may not be immediately doable but it becomes more and more so every year, and the reward is worth it.

The educational value of gaming technologies in teaching history and cultural heritage cannot be understated. An academic paper titled RelicVR: A Virtual Reality Game for Active Exploration of Archeological Relics by Liu et al, states, “The young generation has gradually become impatient with traditional modes of instructions, such as using text or audio explanations, and disengaged with these conventional paradigms of exploration and knowledge acquisition.”3. To illustrate this Liu et al, created the virtual reality game, RelicVR, a game in which players excavate artifacts. This game successfully demonstrates the way gaming technology engages users; however, I will argue that they were unsuccessful in developing an educational experience. Game play is fairly simple, players use tools to excavate an object from a lump of dirt and they must be careful not to hit the object itself while attempting to finish the task before the timer runs out. The setting is a temple, which does not belong to any real ancient culture. It is dilapidated and the scenery brings to mind stories like the Indiana Jones movies. The objects, on the other hand, are modeled after real artifacts, the Arhat (Luohan) sculpture and Sanxingdui bronze head with a gold mask.

This raises multiple red flags. Firstly, by putting a real object in a fake temple, you not only mystify the object itself, but also the culture from which it originates. This also effectively removes it entirely from its cultural and historical context. This setting does not reflect the reality of an archeological dig site and in removing these objects from their context they have removed a valuable educational opportunity. I will not argue that a game like this needs to be completely accurate to the reality of a dig site, players would get bored if they had to go at a realistic pace. But, VR spaces give us a unique opportunity to place things back into their original context and in doing so we can help players develop a better understanding of how these objects fit into not only the past but also our contemporary society. Additionally RelicVR does not provide the player with any additional information about the objects. Players may learn the physical properties of an object, but they will learn little else. Relic VR is first and foremost a game and teaches very little about shared cultural history or archeological practices.

Giving RelicVR the benefit of the doubt, technologies available at the time (2021) and within the budget may have limited the scope of what they were able to accomplish. But, today it is cheaper than ever before to get high quality scans of objects and spaces. Ground penetrating radar, satellite images, lidar scanning, photogrammetry, and so much more means you can reconstruct just about any site. Scenes can also be manually reconstructed from photographs, although that is more expensive and takes significantly more time. But, we have a lot of photographs of archeology sites, so you could even recreate the moment an object was discovered and give the player contextual information about when and where these relics were found. Furthermore, adding simple achievement cards, received upon successful extraction, including a brief synopsis on the object would support the educational value of such a game.

Another gaming possibility for historical education, regarding artifacts, includes a game where the player is a museum curator. Players get to choose which kind of museum to curate for and then the objects within their museum’s archives are randomly generated based on a setlist you’ve scanned for the game which matches their museum choice. In order to make it engaging the goal is to lure people to your museum, similar to already popular attraction building games. Players receive a set budget from which to hire employees, build expansions, and loan objects your museum doesn’t already own. Players would make more money through ticket sales which would allow them to expand. The objects are all based on real life objects, and accompanied by a plaque of educational information. By giving several plaque styles to choose from, the player will end up reading the information as they choose.

Narrative based games may also be engaging to a wider audience. However, when constructing a narrative based historical game you have to be extremely careful about what kind of context you insinuate to your audience. This means, in order to be truly educational and not just entertainment you can't fill in gaps with guesses. According to information scientist Maria Harrington, in regards to virtual learning, “Only when there is high information fidelity combined with the high immersion and embodiment will actual educational learning occur in them. If not, it is fantasy entertainment, a sales pitch, or misinformation or propaganda.”4. A detective game, in which players are provided with only the evidence historians actually have, may solve this issue. For younger audiences levels would consist of historical mysteries we have more confidently solved. And, for older audiences, with more capacity for nuance, unsolved mysteries are appropriate. Unsolved mystery levels can conclude with multiple theories constructed by historians for players to consider. Allowing them to decide on which theories they agree with after game play.

The applications of VR on historical study and education extend beyond game play and can allow us to remotely explore world heritage sites. We have the opportunity to develop experiences for cultural sites we haven’t lost, such as the Egyptian Pyramids, and those we have, such as Mayan settlements. We have detailed maps developed by archeologists of these spaces lost to time, which can be taken from two dimensions and recreated in three dimensions. Examples like unaccessed sections of the Egyptian pyramids and the Terra Cotta Warriors Emperor Qin Shi Huang’s central burial chamber could be scanned without entering them. Technologies like ground penetrating radar could allow us to virtually recreate these kinds of spaces, with minimal detail, for archeological study. These kinds of things could also be used to raise awareness, excitement, and funds for these projects if shared with the general public. Who wouldn’t pay to walk around inside the Egyptian Pyramids?

Furthermore, providing virtual access preserves the sites. For example, the Lascaux Caves in France, which are famous for their ancient cave paintings. People used to visit in droves, which was destroying the natural space. An entire building was built recreating the caves, exactly as they truly are, for people to experience without damaging the source material. Something like this would be wildly less expensive today if done in VR. Places like Carlsbad Caverns, Petroglyphs, and New Mexico Heritage sites could be more safely kept. Especially for cultural sites where the public is not permitted due to preservation concerns, people would enjoy getting a closer look. It doesn't entirely save materials, there will still be physical servers etc, needed to store everything. But, it still takes much less space, material, and labor, while remaining widely accessible. This can be applied to a wealth of other attractions, such as national parks. National park experiences could even include various video game exercises, like canoeing arm exercises, cardio workouts, etc. Scavenger hunts and other such activities may appeal to a younger audience. This not only makes these things more accessible, but it engages a wide audience and creates an archive of these places.

Some have argued that, with digital preservation, when you put an object or site into a VR space, it somehow compromises the original's integrity. This argument is elitist and it diminishes the value of digital archives. Nothing is permanent, but a digital scan is more likely to last longer than the object itself, especially any organic material. This also expands our access to objects that can’t be handled. Either because they are too delicate, or because of toxic properties. VR could allow someone to pick up these things and examine them from all documented angles. Additionally, digital archives can be made accessible around the world without the need to travel, making academic research easier than it ever has been. Julia Graciela Machado et al, asserts, in regards to digitizing historical assets, “More than a technical effort, it is a civic undertaking rooted in the belief that memory, when preserved and shared responsibly, becomes a foundation for justice and collective resilience.”5. Open source digitizing software needs to be further integrated within our archival practices because the doors it opens are far too valuable to ignore. The possibilities this presents for museums are also very exciting.

Museums have the opportunity to build a comprehensive archive of past exhibits. What we currently have in regards to past exhibit practices are photos and descriptions, but VR archives would allow students and professionals to see the exhibit itself, as it was seen by visitors. Human beings are wired to look for a story, we are a narrative focused species. As such we tend to draw conclusions based on even the smallest correlations. Something as simple as how you lay out objects in relation to one another changes the way people understand the historical contexts of what you are showing them. This is the same for art, advertisements, and more. Simply showing a picture of a man next to a bowl of spaghetti causes people to assume he’s hungry. Or place him next to a car and it is assumed he’s going somewhere. And, most of the time we aren't even aware we are doing it. So, having an archive of this kind has vast implications for the field of museum studies. It could allow us to better compare exhibit practices and analyze the ways we tell stories through this complex medium. Having a resource of this kind could change the field of museum studies in ways we cannot even predict.

Additionally, the applications of this extend beyond higher education. By putting this kind of an archive online we would make these exhibits accessible around the world. Museums could make them available for the cost of a museum ticket for one time use or for larger access as a subscription service. Furthermore, doing something like this would not lessen museum visitorship, because it will not only serve as an additional revenue stream but also raise public interest in the museum itself. But, on the other hand for museums like the Louvre, which consistently gets more and more overcrowded every year, visitorship could be curbed without entirely removing public access. Additionally, by selling virtual tickets at a reduced cost individuals are further incentivized to take advantage of this alternative. It will still be wildly popular to visit in person, but this kind of additional service would assist overwhelmed employees. Museums also don’t have to put up any current exhibits immediately. Waiting until an exhibit is taken down and then putting it out digitally would ensure in person ticket sales are uninhibited. Institutions can rotate which past exhibits are available within a subscription service at any given time to encourage continued patronage. And the museum itself will always have the full archive available to themselves for academic purposes. Furthermore, more people may be inclined to purchase a single time ticket or subscription than people would purchase an in person ticket, not because they don't want to visit in person but because of the ease of access. Removing travel costs for consumers unable to afford an in person visit would be extremely popular. This additional revenue could provide more bandwidth to museum programs, especially given that as public cultural institutions, they are consistently undervalued. Extra revenue could be put back into in person programs, curbing additional fears of lessened visitorship. Children’s programs could be expanded as well as public and donor museum events. All of which would further support the museum.

In conclusion, museums and history educators should take another look at implementing VR technologies, now that it is both cheaper and easier than ever before. Revisiting will allow us to take advantage of the benefits of teaching through a game, which gets people more engaged and supports critical thought. Exhibit and cultural site archives would make world heritage both more accessible and support academic research. Travel costs make many museums inaccessible, and although not everyone has access to VR technologies they are much easier to transport and make accessible through library, hospital, and school partnerships, allowing us to provide experiences to hospital patients, promoting wellness, as well as library users and students, promoting learning resources, which would further expand the educational reach of these programs. The financial support this could provide to overlooked cultural institutions would greatly expand our alternative education spaces and support future generations. Furthermore, an archive like this simply does not currently exist. Given our highly technological world and the research implications of such an archive, the longer it doesn’t the more this becomes an egregious oversight. We have the opportunity to delve deeper into the field of museum studies and the most exciting part is that we don't have any way to know exactly what we could learn until we do it. So, let's do it!
